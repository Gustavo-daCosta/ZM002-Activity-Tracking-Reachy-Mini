\section{Results}

\subsection{Description of the Artefact}
The end product is a software application: a working prototype (an instantiation, in Design Science Research terms) that turns the Reachy Mini into a CPU-only activity monitor that answers what it sees with its own body.
It consists of three parts: a layered software architecture that runs the same recognition code in simulation and on the robot, recognition models exported as NumPy-only files, and robot applications built on both.
It addresses the requirements of the software requirements specification (SRS) attached to this article (Section~3.1).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pipeline_architecture.png}
  \caption{Pipeline Architecture.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} represents the artefact's pipeline.
Everything inside the dashed line is the same code in simulation and on the robot; only the camera and the pose estimator differ.
Models are trained offline, or by the coach on the robot, and loaded as \texttt{.npz} files.

The pipeline works in such way that a camera frame goes to a pose estimator that returns 17 COCO keypoints: MoveNet Lightning int8 on the robot, BlazePose on the laptop webcam in simulation.
The keypoints enter a sliding window, are normalised to the torso and summarised as temporal features.
A random forest, evaluated by a NumPy-only tree walk, classifies every window, and a per-class confidence floor turns uncertain windows into \emph{none}.
Each detection drives the feedback: a distinct antenna signature per action, rendered at 50~Hz independently of the 10~FPS vision loop, head and body following that keeps the person in view, and an optional spoken label.

As for architecture and organization, the code is organised by where it must run: a shared core (pose backends, features, models, feedback), robot applications, simulation tools for MuJoCo and a webcam, and training tools, which are the only part that needs scikit-learn.
Dependencies point only into the core, so one model file and one feature implementation serve both environments, and training and inference cannot drift apart.

For the final application, the robot runs action recognition (the six classes, or the CMAS items of Section~\ref{sec:cmas}), wave recognition, head and body tracking, and a coach for children.
The coach guides a child through about 4.5 minutes of recording (squats, arm raises, side bends), trains a personal model on the robot's own CPU while the head keeps following, and then runs a workout with that model.
A preflight tool brings the robot into a known state before every application.

\subsection{Robot Sessions and User Feedback}
The prototype implements the robot as an interactive session partner rather than as a camera that silently records the user. A session is organised as a state machine with an initialisation phase, a welcome, exercise rounds, feedback after each round, and a final celebration. Figure~\ref{fig:session-example} illustrates this flow for a four-round workout. The camera loop continuously processes poses at approximately 10~FPS, while a separate transition thread handles speech, pauses and robot movements. This separation prevents a slow speech or gesture operation from blocking pose detection, repetition counting or person tracking.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/session_example.png}
  \caption{Example exercise session with the Reachy Mini.}
  \label{fig:session-example}
\end{figure}

Two related session types were implemented. In a personalised coach session, the child first introduces themself by name and is guided through a short recording procedure. The robot asks the child to demonstrate \texttt{none}, squats, arm raises and side bends in shuffled rounds. Before recording starts, the system checks whether the head, hips, knees and ankles are visible and asks the child to step back when necessary. Each round is accepted only when enough of the body is visible and sufficient movement is present, an unsuccessful round is repeated or the child is invited to continue. The recorded data contains keypoints rather than video and is stored locally on the robot. After recording, a personalised classifier is trained on the robot's CPU. The robot communicates that it is learning through spoken progress messages and thinking gestures, and then uses the resulting model for the workout.

In the workout session, the robot announces the next exercise, demonstrates it with its head, body or antennas, and prompts the child to perform the requested number of repetitions. The session engine filters recognition to the target exercise, so unrelated movements do not advance the round. A recognised repetition is counted immediately, spoken aloud and accompanied by an antenna beat. When the target is reached, or when the time limit expires, the robot reports the result and moves to the next round. For an arms-up CMAS item, a geometric hold detector measures the duration instead of counting repetitions, the robot gives periodic encouragement while the posture is held. The CMAS session uses the same interaction structure for chair rise, pick-up and arms-up rounds, but the prototype reports recognition and timing rather than a clinical CMAS score.

Feedback is deliberately multimodal. Speech provides explicit instructions, repetition counts, encouragement, timeout messages and completion summaries. Antenna signatures distinguish recognised actions and provide fast non-verbal confirmation, while head and body movements communicate welcome, demonstrations, attention and celebration. The robot follows the user's body with its head and body so that the interaction remains visually directed at the person. A camera overlay can additionally show the current phase, round, remaining time, repetition count, skeleton and the last spoken caption. During framing, the overlay and spoken hints explain which body parts are not visible. During training, it shows elapsed learning time. These channels are complementary: speech explains what to do, movement confirms that the robot noticed the action, and the visual overlay makes the internal session state inspectable when audio is unavailable.

This design can improve the expected home experience in several ways. Immediate, action-specific feedback reduces uncertainty about whether an exercise was detected and makes the system less like an opaque measurement device. Demonstrations and short instructions reduce the need for a parent or clinician to repeat every exercise explanation, while repetition counts and round transitions give the child a clear sense of progress. Praise, playful gestures and a personalised model support the intended role of the robot as an exercise buddy. Framing checks and retry logic make recoverable camera problems explicit instead of silently producing missing measurements. Local keypoint storage also supports privacy by avoiding the need to retain children's video. These are design benefits inferred from the implemented interaction. No user study was conducted, so increased engagement, reduced burden or improved adherence cannot yet be claimed as measured outcomes.

\subsection{Pose-Model Performance}
The benchmark compared BlazePose, MoViNet-A0, TSM-MobileNetV2, ViTPose and MoveNet as candidates for the perception pipeline.
MoveNet Lightning int8 reached 23.80~FPS in the inference benchmark, with a mean pose-inference time of approximately 34~ms on the Reachy Mini; in the complete application the camera, at about 10~FPS, sets the rate.
This was the most suitable result for the CPU-only target, and the model produces the COCO-17 keypoints the recognition code expects.
Cropping each frame around the previous pose, instead of letterboxing the whole wide-angle frame, raised the share of robot frames with a detected person from 52\% to 72\%.
On a replay of a laptop recording with the robot's aspect ratio, it raised the mean keypoint confidence from 0.48 to 0.68.

The comparison also exposed deployment constraints. BlazePose and ViTPose are useful comparison models, but the robot cannot use the available BlazePose MediaPipe binary because it requires AES instructions unavailable on the Reachy's Cortex-A72; ViTPose's detector-and-pose pipeline is intended for the laptop or simulation environment. MoViNet-A0 and TSM-MobileNetV2 were considered for temporal classification rather than selected as replacements for the keypoint-based pipeline. Consequently, MoveNet int8 was used for robot inference, while the shared downstream representation remained model-independent. \subsection{GPU Acceleration Experiments}
The two GPU implementations used LiteRT and WebGPU. In MoveNet, an ARM64 LiteRT runner could be created, while in BlazePose a native C++ library as well as a GPU inspector could be built. The two implementations could be tested in runtime, but both failed at the GPU initialization step.
After further investigations, it was found out that there was a compatibility problem with the prebuilt LiteRT WebGPU accelerator. It included AES instructions, which were not supported by the Reachy Mini's Cortex-A72 CPU and thus caused an illegal instruction problem. The problem appeared to be independent of the camera and model inference, meaning that it was caused by the GPU runtime and not by the models themselves.
Neither of the implementations could thus reach the GPU inference step. The full code of the GPU implementations are available in the /repos section of the repository. Build results are included in Appendix~\ref{app:gpu}.

\subsection{CMAS Items}\label{sec:cmas}
In CMAS mode the prototype recognises that four CMAS items were performed; it does not score them.
Items~12 (chair rise) and~14 (pick-up) are classified by a three-class forest (none, chair rise, pick-up) trained on NTU RGB+D alone (actions \enquote{standing up} and \enquote{pick up}).
Items~7 and~8 (arms straight above the head, and for how long) use a geometric rule on single frames, because a held posture has no movement for a window to learn.
Item~13 (stool step) was left out: no public dataset contains it and none was recorded.

\subsection{Wave Recognition}
On two recorded sessions with our own robot (416 windows), the rule-based detector reached F1 0.811 (precision 1.000, recall 0.682). The NTU-trained forest, which never saw these recordings, reached F1 0.951 and outperformed a forest trained on the sessions themselves (F1 0.884, historical only). On unseen NTU subjects, where the negatives include deliberately confusable gestures (salute, selfie and brushing hair), F1 was 0.556. On HMDB51 movie clips it was 0.065, a deliberately pessimistic external check.
